# How to Research a Small-Business Software Comparison Without Inventing Facts

A product comparison is supposed to make a decision easier. But copying feature lists from marketing pages can produce an article that sounds certain while leaving basic questions unanswered: Does the feature exist in the entry plan? Is the price monthly or annual? Does the software work in the buyer’s country?

A reliable comparison begins by separating **verified facts**, **claims made by a seller**, and **questions that still need testing**.

## Start with a buyer, not a product list

Imagine a small service business choosing a tool to record inquiries and send estimates. The decision criteria might include number of users, mobile access, export formats, privacy controls, integrations, and the total cost over a year. A shop with one employee may prioritize simplicity, while a team with several people may need separate roles and an audit trail.

The comparison should state the audience and the date of research so readers understand why an option was included and how quickly its details might change.

## Build an evidence table first

Before drafting paragraphs, create a table with columns for the product, feature, source URL, page date, verification method, and uncertainty.

| Item to verify | Stronger evidence | Remaining question |
|---|---|---|
| Public price | Vendor’s current official pricing page | Does tax or local currency conversion apply? |
| File export | Official documentation or a tested export | Which plan includes it? |
| Integration | Official API reference and supported regions | Does setup require a separate paid service? |
| Data handling | Current privacy documentation | What retention and deletion controls are available? |

Avoid rounding a monthly cost into an annual claim without multiplying and considering billing terms. Never claim a customer support response time unless a published service-level agreement or direct test supports it.

## Test what you can, describe what you cannot

If a public demo exists, take notes on basic tasks: Can a new user create an estimate? Is an export readable? Does the interface handle an empty field sensibly? Record the date, browser, and steps. If a feature cannot be tested without a paid account, say exactly that instead of guessing.

## Write the conclusion for the reader’s use case

A useful final section says *which type of business* may prefer each option, what to verify before purchasing, and which facts might have changed. It should not label a product “best” because the writer saw a high affiliate commission.

**Takeaway:** a trustworthy buyer’s guide is a trail of evidence plus a clear decision framework. Its credibility comes from showing both what was checked and what remains unknown.
